StableSound

Keeps Bluetooth headphones awake so screen reader speech is not cut off at the start, and lets go of them again so your phone can take over.

Getting started

There is nothing to install. Put StableSound.exe wherever you like and run it. Nothing appears on screen: it puts an icon in the system tray and waits.

Then there are three things to know, and they are enough to use it:

  • Ctrl+Windows+F12 switches it on and off. A rising tone means the headphones are being held awake; a falling tone means they have been let go.
  • It lets go on its own after half a minute of quiet, so your phone can take the headphones. Touching the keyboard brings it back.
  • Ctrl+Windows+F11 opens the settings, where everything above can be changed. F1 there opens this help.

StableSound starts the way you left it. If it was switched on when you last shut down, it is switched on again when you next start, without a tone.

To have it there every time, open the settings and tick Start StableSound when I sign in. With that and the line above, there is nothing left to press: sign in and your headphones are already awake.

The rest of this page is detail. You do not need it to get going.

What it is for

Bluetooth headphones switch their receiver off after a few seconds with nothing playing, to save battery. The next time your screen reader speaks, the first fraction of a second is lost while the headset wakes up again. On a short announcement that can be the whole word.

StableSound stops that by sending the headphones a stream of digital silence, so they never decide there is nothing to listen to. You hear nothing; the headset stays awake.

The catch is that while one machine holds a headset open, nothing else can have it — including a phone paired to the same headphones. So StableSound also lets go: after a set time with nothing playing it closes the stream, and the headphones are free again within a few seconds.

Switching it on and off

Press Ctrl+Windows+F12. You will hear a short two-note tone: rising when keep-alive comes on, falling when it goes off. The falling tone finishes before the headphones are released, so you always hear the whole of it.

Those tones play only for a change you made. When StableSound lets go on its own, wakes itself up again, or starts up in the state you left it in, it does so silently. Automatic tones were tried, and they turned out to be heard far too often to live with.

You can also switch it on and off from the system tray. Press Windows+B, use the arrow keys to reach StableSound, and press Enter. The icon reads either StableSound Headphones awake or StableSound Headphones free, so you can check the state without changing it.

It starts the way you left it

Whichever way you last switched it, that is how StableSound comes back the next time it runs. Leave it on and it is on again at your next sign-in, before anything has spoken. Switch it off by hand — to let your phone keep the headphones — and it stays off until you ask for it.

Only the switch counts. Letting go on the timer is not switching off, so a quiet spell before you shut down does not change what you get next time.

There is no setting for this, because the hotkey already is the setting.

Letting the headphones go

By default StableSound releases the headphones once nothing has played for 30 seconds. Speech counts as something playing, so a release never interrupts you mid-sentence; the countdown starts again every time it hears audio.

You can change how long, and you can change the rule itself, in the settings:

When nothing has played for a while
The default, and the one to want. StableSound listens to what is going out of the device and releases only after the machine has been quiet for the number of seconds you set.
A fixed time after switching on
Releases that many seconds after you switched keep-alive on, whatever is playing. Worth having if you want a guaranteed handover time and do not mind it cutting in.

Once released, the headphones are free within about three seconds, and a phone paired to them can claim them.

Waking up again

By default, touching the keyboard or the mouse brings keep-alive back. The reasoning is that sitting down and touching the machine is a reliable sign that speech is about to be wanted, and the first thing to speak after a quiet spell is exactly what would otherwise be clipped.

Waking never happens after you have switched keep-alive off by hand. That is the whole point of switching it off: your phone can then keep the headphones, and typing will not snatch them back.

If you would rather nothing happened by itself, untick Start keep-alive again when I use the keyboard in the settings. The hotkey then becomes the only thing that starts it.

The system tray icon

Press Windows+B and arrow to StableSound. (Windows itself calls the tray the notification area, if you go looking for it in its own settings.)

  • Enter switches keep-alive on or off.
  • The Applications key, or Shift+F10, opens the menu.

The menu holds: keep the headphones awake or release them, Settings, Help, Open the log file, and Exit StableSound. Everything in it can also be reached another way, so you never have to go through the menu if you would rather not.

The settings, one by one

Press Ctrl+Windows+F11, or choose Settings from the system tray menu. It is an ordinary Windows dialog: Tab moves between controls, Enter accepts, Escape cancels, and your usual hotkey goes on working while it is open.

Nothing is saved until you press OK.

Keeping the headphones awake

Output device to keep awake
Leave it on Default output (follow Windows) unless you have a reason not to. That holds whatever Windows is currently playing through, and follows it when that changes.
Naming a device instead is for when you switch between outputs and only one of them is the problem. If the named device is not there when StableSound needs it, it falls back to the default rather than doing nothing.
Keep-alive signal to send it
Leave it on Digital silence. It was enough on every headset tested, and it is the quietest thing that works.
The other two are for a headset that ignores silence. Silence with a tick each second adds an inaudible blip. Inaudible tone is a last resort: it is genuinely playing sound, so it forces the fixed-time release rule — the quiet-detection rule could never fire.
Let go of the headphones
The release rule, described under Letting the headphones go.
Let go after this many seconds
How long. 30 is the default. Shorter hands the headphones over sooner; longer means fewer wake-ups when you come back to the machine.

Waking up again

Start keep-alive again when I use the keyboard
On by default. See Waking up again.

Tones

Play a tone when I switch keep-alive on or off
On by default. Only ever for something you did.
Tone volume (percent)
10 by default, which testing settled on as noticeable without being intrusive. The tones play through the headphones being kept awake, not through the computer's speakers, so they are as loud as the headset is.

Hotkeys

Hotkey to switch keep-alive on and off
ctrl+win+f12 by default.
To change it, type one or more of ctrl, alt, shift and win, then one key, joined by plus signs. Order, spacing and capitals do not matter, so Win + Ctrl + F12 is the same thing. A key on its own is refused: with no modifier it would be taken away from every other program on the machine.
Also use a hotkey to open these settings
On by default. Once your settings are the way you want them you may prefer to untick this and hand the combination back to the rest of the machine; the system tray menu still opens the settings.
Hotkey to open these settings
ctrl+win+f11 by default.

Log file

Write a log file, so a problem can be looked into later
Off by default, so StableSound leaves nothing behind on a machine that is working fine. See The log file.
Detailed logging, for troubleshooting
Adds what the sound level was when audio started and stopped, and how long each device took to open. Worth turning on only when somebody is helping you look into something.

Starting up

Start StableSound when I sign in
Adds StableSound to the programs Windows starts for you, under your own account only. Unticking it removes it again.

StableSound asks Windows for a scheduled task that runs when you sign in. That matters for one reason: a task runs at the sign-in itself, while the other way of starting programs — the list Windows keeps in the registry — is not reached until the desktop is ready, after every other program set to start with you. On a slowish machine that has meant about half a minute, which is half a minute of your screen reader being clipped.

If Windows refuses the task, StableSound falls back to the registry list so that it still starts, and tells you it has done so. Either way this tickbox is read from Windows rather than remembered, so it can never claim StableSound will start when it will not.

The buttons

OK
Saves and applies. A changed hotkey takes effect immediately, without a restart.
Cancel
Changes nothing. Escape does the same.
Help
Opens this page. F1 does the same.
Open the log file
Opens the log in whatever you read text files with.
Exit StableSound
Closes StableSound altogether, releasing the headphones and giving up its hotkeys. Anything you have typed into the dialog but not yet accepted with OK is discarded.

The log file

StableSound can keep a plain text log of what it did and when: keep-alive on and off and why, devices opening and closing, hotkeys claimed, settings changed.

It exists because this is a program whose behaviour cannot be watched while it happens. Reading its output aloud would itself make sound, and sound is the very thing being measured. A log read afterwards, while the headset is quiet, is the only way to see what actually went on.

It is off by default. Turn it on in the settings when something needs looking into, and off again afterwards. The file is stablesound.log, in the folder described under Where StableSound keeps its files, and you can open it from the settings dialog or the system tray menu.

When something goes wrong

The hotkey does nothing

Another program has claimed the same combination. Windows gives a hotkey to whoever asks first and tells everyone else no, so StableSound says so at startup when this happens.

There is a second, harder case: a program that hooks the keyboard can intercept a combination before Windows ever offers it to us, and then nothing is reported because nothing was refused. This is known to happen with Ctrl+Windows+semicolon and Leasey. If a combination does nothing and no message appeared, try a different one — adding or removing Shift is usually enough.

My screen reader says the hotkey name before it works

That is the screen reader echoing a command key, and it happens before StableSound is told anything at all: a global hotkey reaches us only after the screen reader has already seen the keystroke. There is nothing StableSound can do about it. In JAWS the setting that governs it is "speak command keys", which is global — turning it off silences every other command key too, so it is usually not worth it.

Speech is still being clipped

Check that keep-alive is actually on: press Windows+B and arrow to the icon, which reads Headphones awake or Headphones free. If it is on and speech is still clipped, the headset may be ignoring pure silence. Try Silence with a tick each second, and then Inaudible tone, in the settings.

Also check that the right device is being held. If the settings name a device that is not the one you are listening on, set it back to Default output (follow Windows).

My phone will not take the headphones

Something has to release them first. Either wait for the release time to pass with nothing playing, or press Ctrl+Windows+F12 to release them straight away — you will hear the falling tone, and the phone can claim them a couple of seconds later.

If keep-alive keeps coming back on by itself while you are still using the computer, that is the waking feature. Switching off by hand always sticks; waking only ever follows an automatic release.

It was already holding the headphones when I signed in

That is deliberate: StableSound comes back in whatever state you left it, and it was on. If you would rather start free, switch it off by hand with Ctrl+Windows+F12 before you finish for the day, and it will start off from then on.

It takes too long to start when I sign in

Check that StableSound got the scheduled task rather than the fallback. The quickest way is the log: turn logging on, sign out and back in, and read the line beginning autostart: near the top. It says which of the two is in force.

If it says the registry Run key, StableSound will keep trying to move up to a task each time it starts, so it is worth signing out once more before concluding anything. If it stays on the Run key, Windows is refusing the task on this machine and the line says what it said.

If it says the scheduled task and you are still waiting, the remaining delay is not StableSound's to remove. Windows itself pauses at the start of a sign-in before it lets startup programs run, and there is a single Windows setting that removes that pause for all of them at once. StableSound will not change it for you — it is a change to your whole sign-in, made on one program's behalf — but it exists, and you can look for it yourself.

It says StableSound is already running

Because it is — most likely started for you when you signed in. Only one copy can run at a time; two would fight over the same hotkey and the same headphones. The message names the hotkeys the running copy is using.

Antivirus and SmartScreen warnings

StableSound is not signed with a code-signing certificate, and Windows treats unsigned programs it has not seen before with suspicion. The first time you run it you may get a Windows protected your PC screen from SmartScreen. Choosing More info and then Run anyway gets past it, and you should not see it again on that machine.

An antivirus program may also object. A small program that opens audio devices, registers global hotkeys and can start itself at sign-in matches the shape of things that are worth being suspicious of, and heuristics do not know why it is doing them. If yours quarantines StableSound, its own interface will have a way to report a false positive.

Both are reasonable defences behaving as designed. Only download StableSound from its own releases page, and if a copy came from anywhere else, do not trust it.

Where StableSound keeps its files

StableSound is one file, StableSound.exe, and that is the only one you ever have to copy or keep. This help, the settings dialog and the icon are all inside it.

What it writes for itself goes alongside the program:

  • stablesound.conf — the settings.
  • stablesound.log — the log, if you have turned it on.
  • stablesound-help.html — this page, written out fresh each time you open the help.

If the folder StableSound is in cannot be written to — Program Files, for instance, or a read-only drive — both move to %APPDATA%\StableSound instead.

The settings file is safe to edit by hand if you would rather; StableSound reads it at startup. A setting it cannot make sense of is corrected rather than obeyed, and the correction is written to the log.

One line in it, keep_alive, is a memory rather than a setting: it is how StableSound knows what state to come back in, and it rewrites that line itself every time you switch keep-alive on or off.

StableSound writes nothing else. The one thing it puts outside its own folder is what makes it start when you sign in, and only if you ask for it: a scheduled task called StableSound, or an entry in the registry's startup list if Windows will not give it the task. Unticking Start StableSound when I sign in removes whichever it made. Delete the exe and the three files above and nothing of it is left.
